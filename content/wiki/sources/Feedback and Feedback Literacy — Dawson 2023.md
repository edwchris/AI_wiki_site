## Summary

Workshop delivered by Professor Phillip Dawson (Deakin University / CRADLE) at King's College London. Covers the contemporary understanding of effective feedback, a model of student feedback literacy, and practical strategies for teacher feedback literacy. Central argument: effective feedback is defined by its effects — what students do with it — not by the quality of information the teacher provides.

---

## The Shift: From Information Delivery to Process

Traditional view: feedback = writing high-quality comments and "blasting" them to students.

Contemporary definition (process view):
> "Feedback [is] a process in which learners make sense of information about their performance and use it to enhance the quality of their work or learning strategies."

Key implication: if students never access or act on feedback, there is no feedback — only wasted effort.

Evidence of the problem:
- Access rates for written feedback on LMS can be under 10%
- Audio-visual/screencast feedback has higher access rates than text
- In modules with no follow-on assessment, developmental feedback is essentially wasted

**Dylan Wiliam quote:** "Feedback should be more work for the recipient than it is for the donor."

## Student Feedback Literacy

Carless & Boud model — four components:

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Appreciating feedback** | Connoisseurship: identifying what is credible, useful, and worth acting on — including deciding NOT to act on some feedback |
| **Managing affect** | Making productive use of emotions; neither suppressing nor being paralysed by them; recognising that first-read reactions differ from action-planning reads |
| **Making judgments** | Evaluating feedback quality; evaluative judgment about the quality of your own work relative to the standard |
| **Taking action** | Actually doing something with the feedback — the only component that produces learning effects |

> [!important]
> A highly feedback-literate student not only takes on feedback — they also consciously decide *not* to act on some feedback. Uptake alone is not a reliable indicator of literacy.

### Strategies to develop student feedback literacy
- Require specific feedback requests at submission (reject submissions without them)
- Have students self-assess using the same rubric before receiving teacher assessment
- Compare teacher assessment with self-assessment; provide detailed comments only where judgments diverge
- Build feedback dialogues into assessment design (not just end-of-module comments)
- Make feedback-seeking compulsory (not optional office hours)
- Teach students to recognise informal feedback (conversations, emails) as feedback
- Public health model: mainstream these practices for all students, not as remediation for struggling students

## Teacher Feedback Literacy

Dawson & Boud ("What feedback literate teachers do") — 19 competencies across three levels:

| Level | Focus |
|-------|-------|
| **Macro** | Program-level feedback design; qualification frameworks |
| **Meso** | Module-level design and sequencing of feedback events |
| **Micro** | Individual feedback comments and practices on specific assignments |

> [!important]
> The meso level has the most leverage but is most neglected. Better micro-level comments are of no use if students never access them. Meso-level design (timing, sequencing, cycles) drives uptake.

### Four key meso-level competencies (from the paper):
1. Maximising effects of limited opportunities for feedback
2. Organising timing, location, and sequencing of feedback events
3. Designing for feedback dialogues and cycles
4. Designing to intentionally prompt student action

### Practical advice for feedback-literate teachers:
- **Front-load developmental feedback**: most effort early in module (where it can have an effect), not on final task (where it can't)
- **Separate marks from feedback**: giving both simultaneously causes students to ignore feedback and look only at the mark. Give feedback first, marks later
- **Rubric comparison**: students self-assess, teacher assesses, compare discrepancies — only write comments where assessments differ (workload negative)
- **Feedback design scales** better than feedback provision: upfront design investments don't require linearly more time as class sizes grow
- **Authentic feedback**: replicate the real world, where you *cannot* ignore your supervisor's feedback (unlike in HE where you often can)

## Feedback Sandwich Critique
The feedback sandwich (positive–negative–positive) is critiqued because:
- Some students see through the ritual and only attend to the critical middle
- Others take the sandwich at face value and conclude they are doing well
- Neither response is educationally productive
- Better: direct, honest feedback with explicit coaching framing

## Peer Feedback vs. Peer Assessment
- **Peer feedback** = students provide developmental comments on each other's work → strong pedagogical value; develops evaluative judgment
- **Peer assessment** = students assign grades to each other's work → more contested; validity concerns
- Reciprocity model (withhold teacher feedback until peer feedback is submitted) can increase formative engagement
- Students often undervalue peer feedback relative to expert feedback — hard to overcome, but requiring active processing (e.g. "what will you do differently?") helps
