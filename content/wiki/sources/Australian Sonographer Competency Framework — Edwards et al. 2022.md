## Summary

Reports the development of a contemporary, national competency framework for Australian sonographers via a Delphi study. Led by a multi-institutional team of academics and professional body representatives; 55-member expert panel across all Australian states and domains of practice; 6 survey rounds achieving 70% consensus. Replaces the Australasian Sonography Association (ASA) standards published in 2011. The framework extends from student level through to expert practitioner — filling a critical gap left by the prior framework, which only covered novice to competent.

---

## Four-Component Framework

| Component | Description | Application |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| **1. Sonographer competency standards** | 77 performance criteria, 307 entry-level cues, 42 advanced cues; 5 units (see below) | Regulatory standards, curriculum design, accreditation |
| **2. Sonographer knowledge** | 125 knowledge items (entry-level); 49 advanced; domain-specific (abdominal, OB, MSK, etc.) | Curriculum content, assessment |
| **3. Sonographer attitudes** | 16 consensus attitudes (see below) | Personal development, professionalism |
| **4. Holistic competency matrix** | 40 cells × 5 Dreyfus levels × 8 practice aspects; less granular, captures complex integration | Performance management, career progression |

---

## Five Competency Units (Standards Component)

| Unit | Elements |
|------|---------|
| **1. Professional and Ethical Practitioner** | Ethical practice; patient dignity; accountability; societal commitment; patient advocacy; profession advancement |
| **2. Lifelong Learner** | Critical/reflective thinking; ongoing professional learning; facilitating learning in clinical environment |
| **3. Safety and Risk Manager** | Safe practice; patient safety; quality assurance; workplace safety |
| **4. Communicator and Collaborator** | Patient/carer communication; inter-professional collaboration |
| **5. Sonographic Practitioner** | Anatomy/physiology/pathology application; procedure strategy development; safe implementation; documentation |

> [!important]
> Unit 5 is where AI is most active, but its footprint within Unit 5 is uneven. **5.4 (documentation)** is the most AI-amenable element. **5.3 (implementation)** is split: AI assists with acquisition mechanics (probe navigation, plane detection) but the patient safety dimension — monitoring patient response, adapting mid-scan, managing complications — remains human. **5.2 (procedure strategy)** is robustly human: choosing the approach, probe, and patient-specific adaptations is K-level clinical judgment AI does not supply. **5.1 (anatomy/pathology knowledge)** is AI-assisted but human-applied. The AI-resistant core spans all of Units 1–4 and the clinical judgment sub-elements within Unit 5. See [[AI in Real-Time Procedural Imaging — Framework Paper Analysis 2026-06-27]].

## Holistic Competency Matrix — Eight Practice Aspects

| Aspect | SRK Level | AI Disruption |
|--------|-----------|---------------|
| Applied knowledge | K-level | AI provides rule-based suggestions; K-level understanding of *why* remains human |
| Psychomotor skills | S-level | AI guidance tools bypass psychomotor practice → skill may not develop |
| Standard of work | R→K | AI can check against standards (R), but expert standard-setting requires K-level |
| Autonomy | K-level | AI may create dependency that undermines autonomous practice |
| Coping with complexity | K-level | Precisely where AI fails; novel/emergency cases require human K-level reasoning |
| Perception of content | S→R | Pattern recognition partially automatable; expert perceptual gestalt is not |
| Attitudes to learning | — | Not automated; may be undermined if AI removes challenge |
| Attitudes toward self/colleagues/patients | — | Relational, affective — irreplaceable |

## Sixteen Consensus Attitudes

Sense of responsibility · Patience · Accountability · Humility · Valuing professionalism · Respect of self · Open-mindedness · Resilience · Respect for others · Commitment to excellence · Open to learning and teaching · Self-awareness of personal level of expertise · Mindfulness of patient well-being · Emotional intelligence and empathy · Time awareness with patients · Communication skill development

> [!note]
> These 16 attitudes form the AI-proof residual of professional identity. They align precisely with what the relational residual argument in the framework paper analysis identifies as the "remaining irreplaceable contribution" of the specialist sonographer. See [[Caring Relationship Between Sonographer and Patient — Van Der Westhuizen et al. 2020]].

## Dreyfus Model Alignment

The holistic competency matrix is explicitly structured around the Dreyfus & Dreyfus (1980) 5-level model:
- **Novice → Advanced Beginner → Competent**: student assessment and clinical goal-setting
- **Proficient → Expert**: performance management and career planning for accredited sonographers

This maps to the [[Skills Rules and Knowledge]] framework:
- Novice/Advanced Beginner = K-level dominant (must analyse each situation deliberately)
- Competent/Proficient = R-level dominant (rules compiled from experience)
- Expert = S-level dominant (automatic, fluid performance from compiled patterns)

The competency framework thus implicitly encodes SRK level expectations at each career stage — and AI disruption of S-level skill development directly undermines the progression from competent to expert.

## Key Limitations

- Students and patients not represented in the expert panel (design decision)
- 34 cues and 37 knowledge items did not reach 70% consensus — divergent professional opinion on advanced vs. entry-level classification
- Framework reflects the profession "as it currently stands" — rapid AI development will require ongoing review
- Framework does not address how competency demonstration differs when AI tools are used vs. when working independently
