**Citation:** Thomas, S., O'Loughlin, K., & Clarke, J. (2017). The 21st century sonographer: Role ambiguity in communicating an adverse outcome in obstetric ultrasound. *Cogent Medicine*, 4(1), 1373903. DOI: 10.1080/2331205X.2017.1373903

**Raw file:** `Thomas, O'Loughlin, Clarke - 2017 - The 21st c.pdf`

## Summary
Qualitative study examining the professional role ambiguity sonographers face when required to communicate adverse findings in obstetric ultrasound — a task normally associated with medical practitioners. Frames this as a 21st-century professional challenge arising from expanding sonographer scope without commensurate role clarity. Relevant context for understanding what "non-technical" professional competencies are needed and how AI may affect them.

## Core Finding
Sonographers occupy a professional space "between" diagnostic and clinical roles in obstetric scanning:
- They detect abnormalities; they are the first to know
- Communication protocols vary widely (some sites require immediate escalation; others permit sonographer disclosure)
- Role ambiguity creates stress, moral distress, and inconsistent patient experience

## The Role Ambiguity Problem
- Sonographers are trained for image acquisition and interpretation (technical role)
- In adverse outcome situations, they are thrust into a communication-with-patients role (clinical role) without adequate protocol support or training
- This gap is not unique to Australia but particularly prominent where sonographers have greater diagnostic scope

## Implications for AI
- AI detection of fetal abnormalities may surface adverse findings more prominently (earlier, more consistently flagged)
- If AI generates a preliminary finding before sonographer has assessed the case → who is responsible for the communication pathway?
- AI could exacerbate role ambiguity: sonographer may be confronted with AI findings they feel obligated to communicate before full clinical review
- Connects to accountability concerns in [[The Role of Patient Outcomes in Moral Responsibility — Edwards et al. 2025]]
