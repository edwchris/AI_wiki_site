**Citation:** Wears, R. L., & Berg, M. (2005). Computer technology and clinical work: still waiting for Godot. *JAMA*, 293(10), 1261–1263. DOI: 10.1001/jama.293.10.1261

**Raw file:** `jed50009.pdf`

**Type:** Viewpoint/editorial — published in a JAMA theme issue alongside systematic reviews of CPOE and clinical decision support systems

## The Argument
The title's Beckett reference is the thesis: the promised transformation of clinical work by computers has never arrived, and the waiting continues. After decades of enthusiasm, clinical computing has consistently underperformed its projected benefits.

## Core Claims

**1. Clinical work is irreducibly complex**
Clinical work is not a series of discrete decisions amenable to algorithmic support — it is highly contextual, improvisational, and distributed. Technology designed for idealized clinical workflows fails when it meets actual work.

**2. The mismatch between design assumptions and practice**
Clinical IT systems encode assumptions about how work *should* happen, not how it *does* happen. This creates friction, workarounds, and new error modes rather than eliminating the old ones.

**3. Promised benefits require conditions that do not exist**
Error reduction, efficiency gains, and decision support improvements all depend on clean data, cooperative workflows, and users who engage as designed. These conditions are rarely met.

**4. Technology as a lens that distorts**
Focusing on technology as the solution draws attention away from the systemic and organisational factors that actually drive clinical error — staffing, culture, communication, training.

## Historical Significance
Published in 2005, this editorial preceded:
- The US meaningful use legislation (2009) that mandated EHR adoption
- The wave of EHR dissatisfaction documented in the 2010s (physician burnout, alert fatigue)
- The current AI-in-medicine discourse repeating the same promises

> This paper is a direct ancestor of the current AI critique literature. Replace "CPOE" and "clinical decision support" with "AI" and the argument is indistinguishable from contemporary critiques — see [[Human-Centered Evaluation of AI in Diabetic Retinopathy Clinics — Beede et al. 2020]], [[Is AI Ground Truth Really True — Lebovitz, Levina & Lifshitz-Assaf 2021]], and [[Implementing AI Decision Support in Radiology — JMIR 2026]].

## Connection to Ironies of Automation
Wears & Berg's argument parallels [[Ironies of Automation]]:
- Both note that technology designed to simplify work creates new complexity
- Both argue that the human factors are the hard problem, not the technology
- Both were written before their respective technology waves peaked — and both remain unheeded
