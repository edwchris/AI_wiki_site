## Summary

Keynote delivered by Professor Phillip Dawson (Deakin University / CRADLE) at the ATU DigitalEd 2025 conference. The central claim: AI can now do what we ask students to do in many assessments, so assessment must change — not by layering AI instructions onto existing tasks (discursive change), but by redesigning the structure of assessment itself (structural change). Presents TEXA guidance he co-developed as a framework, then offers practical "thought tools" for educators.

---

## Core Claim: Assessment Needs to Change

- AI can complete most unsupervised take-home tasks to a passing standard with good prompting and iterative refinement
- Students are already using AI in assessment (HEPI UK 2024 survey; Australian data, n≈6000)
- "Most of the time when someone thinks AI can't do their assessment and then they ask me or Thomas [Corbin], it's not that hard for us to get AI to do the task"
- AI progress shows no sign of stopping (cite: 2021 "A Brief History of AI" — previously unsolvable tasks are now routine)
- Designing tasks "AI can't do now" is not a viable long-term strategy

---

## Two Guiding Principles (TEXA Guidance)

Co-developed with Jason Lodge, Sarah Howard, and Margaret Beerman for Australia's higher education regulator (TEXA):

| Principle | Focus |
|-----------|-------|
| **1. Assessment for Learning** | Equip students to participate ethically and actively in a society where AI is ubiquitous |
| **2. Assessment of Learning** | Forming trustworthy judgments about student learning in a time of AI requires multiple, inclusive, and contextualized approaches |

> [!important]
> Taking only one principle produces arguments. Principle 1 alone ignores the credential-validity problem. Principle 2 alone ignores the world students graduate into. Both must be held simultaneously.

---

## Five Propositions ("A Compass, Not a Map")

All begin with "Assessment should emphasize...":

| # | Proposition |
|---|------------|
| 1 | **Appropriate authentic engagement with AI** — represent how AI is actually used in the discipline/profession |
| 2 | **A systemic approach to program assessment** — zoom out from individual tasks to the full qualification; how do we know across the whole suite what someone can do? |
| 3 | **The process of learning** — not just the product; process check-ins, logs, supervision dialogues |
| 4 | **Opportunities to work with each other and AI** — collaborative tasks; students must graduate able to work with peers |
| 5 | **Security at meaningful points** — ~half-dozen high-confidence assessments per degree; not an exam at the end of every module |

Dawson's ranking: Proposition 5 (security) is his personal priority — "a dismal world, but without it you can't assure learning."

---

## Personal Addenda to TEXA Guidance

1. **Cheating is not a useful framing** — too much ethical ambiguity with AI; students using AI authentically to what practitioners do shouldn't be labelled cheaters. Better: focus on validity.
2. **Validity underpins everything** — "assessing what we mean to assess"; Corbin et al. (forthcoming) "Validity matters more than cheating."
3. **Substituting all tasks with "critique AI output" is dangerous** — this is a *different* learning outcome (evaluative judgment) which may or may not be what you want. You can't give out "AI degrees" where the only competency assessed is judging AI.

## Thought Tools for Redesigning Assessment

### 1. Change the Outcomes
If outcomes are obsolete, ditch them. "Are we really talking about assessment or not?" — many debates about assessment are actually debates about what *should* be assessed.

### 2. Validity Over Cheating
From Corbin et al. (forthcoming):
> "Does student use of AI get in the way of — or support — our ability to judge if the student has met the outcome?"

Validity framing unifies: cheating hurts validity; but so does test anxiety and remote proctoring instability. Surveillance solutions can create validity problems of their own.

### 3. Future Authentic Assessment
What does authentic practice look like in this discipline *in the future*, including likely AI use? Deakin's "Future Focused AI" program funds academics to investigate their discipline's real-world AI practices.

### 4. Scaffolding vs. Reverse Scaffolding
- **Scaffolding approach**: let students use AI until they can do it themselves — risk: they never learn to do it themselves
- **Reverse scaffolding**: students can only use AI for things they can already do themselves (e.g., learn referencing by hand before using EndNote)

### 5. Zone of Proximal Development
> "AI tools for production don't respect the zone of proximal development. Their orientation is towards making me happy by doing it for me."

A teacher supports the learner through what they can do with help → what they can do alone. AI production tools don't have that orientation. Pedagogical AI tools (designed for learning, not production) are different.

### 6. Cognitive Offloading
From cognitive load theory:
- **Intrinsic load**: the core cognitive work inseparable from the learning outcome — cannot be offloaded
- **Extraneous load**: busy work that doesn't contribute to the outcome — offloading to AI may be fine

Question: what is extraneous in this assessment, and is it okay for students to offload it?

### 7. Evaluative Judgment in an AI Age
Students must graduate capable of judging:
- Their own work
- AI-produced work
- Peers' work
- Whether a practice meets professional standards

This is increasingly essential and is itself worth assessing — but cannot be the *only* thing assessed (see addenda above).

## Structural vs. Discursive Change

| Type | Description | Limitation |
|------|-------------|-----------|
| **Discursive** | Address AI through instructions: "use AI for editing only," AI-scale level designations | Cannot be enforced; students who know this will use AI anyway; hurts validity |
| **Structural** | Address AI through unavoidable design changes: in-class assessment, oral vivas, process check-ins | High confidence; workload-intensive but scalable with economies of scale |

> [!important]
> "If you aren't supervising, you can't be sure how AI was or wasn't used. You can set guidelines. The guidelines are great, but you shouldn't set restrictions you can't enforce."

Advice: secure ~half-dozen tasks per degree program; **accept that all others will see significant AI use.**

## Swiss Cheese Security Model

Multiple imperfect interventions layered together are more robust than any single intervention:
- Oral vivas / interactive oral assessment
- In-class supervised tasks
- Process logs and check-ins
- Placement-based assessment
- Peer feedback as authenticity indicator

Each layer has holes; overlap closes gaps. Drawn from Reason's Swiss cheese model of accident causation.

---

## On Over-Assessment
Assessment volume should be determined by: "what information do I need to make a high quality judgment about what the student is capable of?" — not by workload or tradition. Long-form tasks can often be shortened without reducing validity.
