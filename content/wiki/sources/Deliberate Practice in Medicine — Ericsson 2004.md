# Deliberate Practice in Medicine — Ericsson 2004

**Full citation:** Ericsson KA. Deliberate practice and the acquisition and maintenance of expert performance in medicine and related domains. *Acad Med*. 2004;79(10 Suppl):S70–S81.

**DOI:** 10.1097/00001888-200410001-00022

**Author:** K. Anders Ericsson, Department of Psychology, Florida State University

---

## What it is

A comprehensive review by Ericsson — originator of deliberate practice theory — applying his framework to the acquisition and maintenance of expert performance specifically in medicine. Published as a supplement to *Academic Medicine*. Directly challenges the assumption that clinical experience automatically produces expertise.

## Core framework: Deliberate practice

Expert performance requires a distinct type of practice — not mere repetition, but **deliberate practice** characterised by:

1. **Defined task goal** — specific, challenging performance target beyond current level
2. **Immediate informative feedback** — accurate information on whether the goal was achieved
3. **Repeated opportunities** — sufficient repetition with full concentration

> [!important] Simply accumulating clinical experience does NOT reliably produce expertise. Physicians who see many patients without targeted feedback often reach a performance plateau early and then stabilise or decline.

Ericsson distinguishes:
- **Naive practice**: repeated performance without feedback or reflection → automaticity → arrested development
- **Deliberate practice**: targeted challenge + immediate feedback + repetition → continued improvement

## Evidence base

### Chess and music (foundational studies)
- Chase & Simon (1973): Chess masters have ~50,000 chunk patterns from deliberate study — not just talent
- Sloboda et al.: violin students at music schools — hours of solitary practice (not total playing) predicted skill level
- 10-year rule: no instance found of reaching international level in any domain in less than 10 years

### Medical performance (key findings)

**Perceptual diagnosis**
- Radiologists: Training improved accuracy for detecting pulmonary embolism; experience alone led to *decline* in performance over time without continued challenge
- ECG reading: expert-ranked "best teachers" did not outperform average physicians in actual diagnostic accuracy (Elstein et al.)

**Patient diagnosis**
- Studies of peer-nominated "expert" clinicians vs average physicians: **experts did not systematically outperform non-experts** on retrospective diagnostic cases
- Working diagnosis formed after <3 items of patient information in 75–80% of cases
- Efficiency = good medicine in common cases; efficiency = premature closure in complex cases

**Surgery**
- Experienced surgeons: complication rates higher for surgeons who graduated decades ago vs recent graduates (for procedures where evidence has improved)
- Coronary artery bypass surgery: outcomes plateau or degrade with time unless surgeons actively maintain deliberate practice via simulation or supervised training

### Expert performance maintenance

- Older expert musicians can maintain performance into 60s–70s if they maintain deliberate practice levels
- If practice reduces, performance degrades — age alone does not explain decline
- Application to medicine: active maintenance of diagnostic skill requires deliberate challenge, not just clinical volume

## Implications for AI and skill acquisition

> [!important] When AI automates perceptual or diagnostic tasks, the deliberate practice opportunities that normally arise from clinical work disappear. This is the mechanism behind the deskilling effects documented by Cabitza et al.

- Medical students/trainees who learn with AI assistance may never develop independent baseline competence
- "Experience" with AI-assisted practice does not provide the same deliberate practice framework as unassisted practice with feedback
- Simulation-based training offers a partial solution: creates deliberate practice conditions in controlled settings
