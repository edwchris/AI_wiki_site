**Citation:** Edwards, C., Murphy, A., Gunn, T., Singh, A., Arruzza, E., Makanjee, C., Geritz, E., & Chamunyonga, C. (2026). Measuring AI preparedness in health professions education: Evidence from a national survey of medical radiation science students and new graduates. *Radiography*, 32(5), 103438.

**Raw file:** `EBSCO-FullText-04_09_2026.pdf` (downloaded 9 April 2026)

## Summary
National cross-sectional survey benchmarking perceived AI preparedness among Australian medical radiation science (MRS) students and new graduates. Identifies gaps between ethical orientation and foundational AI knowledge, with implications for curriculum design.

## Methodology
- Tool: Medical Artificial Intelligence Readiness Scale for Medical Students (MAIRS-MS)
- Four domains assessed: **Cognition**, **Ability**, **Vision**, **Ethics**
- Population: Final-year students and recent graduates from accredited Australian MRS programs
- Design: Cross-sectional online survey
- Quantitative + qualitative analysis

## Key Quantitative Findings
- **Ethics scored highest** across all domains
- **Cognition scored lowest** — weakest foundational AI knowledge
- Formal AI education → significantly higher preparedness (79.1 ± 12.0 vs 70.5 ± 13.0, p = 0.01)
- Clinical exposure to AI tools → significantly higher preparedness (80.1 ± 11.2 vs 69.5 ± 13.0, p < 0.01)

## Qualitative Themes (4)
1. **Variable confidence and readiness**: wide spread across cohort; many feel unprepared despite clinical placement
2. **Ethical responsibility and professional identity**: strong ethical orientation; AI seen as a professional accountability challenge, not just a technical tool
3. **Navigating different "styles" of AI**: students distinguish clinical AI (diagnostic tools) from generative AI (ChatGPT-type tools) — require different competencies
4. **Structural misalignment**: university AI teaching lags behind what students encounter in clinical placement → gap between formal education and real-world AI practice

## Implications
- Curricula must strengthen **foundational AI knowledge (Cognition)** — currently the weakest domain
- Authentic clinical AI experiences are essential for preparedness — simulate AI tool use in training, not just classroom coverage
- Professional accountability (what to do when AI is wrong) must be made explicit in curriculum → connects to [[The Role of Patient Outcomes in Moral Responsibility — Edwards et al. 2025]]
- Ethics alone is insufficient — ethical orientation without AI knowledge may limit critical appraisal of AI outputs
