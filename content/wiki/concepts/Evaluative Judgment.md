# Evaluative Judgment

The ability to make decisions about the quality of work — your own work, the work of others, or the output of AI. Developed primarily by David Boud and colleagues; extended by [[Phillip Dawson]] and Margaret Beerman for the AI age.

---

## Definition

> Evaluative judgment is your ability to make decisions about the quality of your own work, the work of others, and the work of AI.

Students must graduate capable of judging:
- Whether their own work meets professional standards
- Whether a peer's work is good enough
- Whether AI-generated content is accurate, appropriate, and useful
- Whether a practice or outcome is safe (particularly in clinical disciplines)

---

## How Evaluative Judgment Develops

Evaluative judgment develops through **enactment** — through repeatedly making judgments about the quality of work:
- Self-assessment against a rubric (then comparing with teacher assessment)
- Peer feedback (constructing evaluative comments on others' work)
- Evaluating exemplars of different quality levels
- Iterative feedback cycles where you assess, receive feedback, reassess

> [!important]
> Providing students with rubrics and exemplars is not sufficient. They must *use* them to make judgments and see the consequences of those judgments. The active practice of judging is what builds the capability.

---

## Evaluative Judgment and Feedback Literacy

[[Feedback Literacy]] requires evaluative judgment at multiple stages:
- **Appreciating feedback**: judging whether feedback is credible and useful
- **Making judgments**: assessing the quality of your own work relative to the standard
- Deciding which feedback to act on and which to reject

In Dawson's rubric-comparison method: teacher and student assess independently; discrepancies reveal where evaluative judgment is underdeveloped. This is simultaneously a feedback design strategy and an evaluative judgment development strategy.

---

## Evaluative Judgment in the AI Age

This is where evaluative judgment becomes **critical infrastructure** for higher education:

1. **Judging AI output**: if students (and graduates) use AI to produce work, they must be able to judge whether that output is good enough, accurate, and appropriate — or whether it hallucinated, oversimplified, or missed the disciplinary standard.

2. **AI will judge their work**: AI-based grading and screening tools are increasingly deployed in professional contexts. Students need to understand how to prepare for and respond to AI evaluation.

3. **The substitution trap**: replacing all assessment with "evaluate AI output" only assesses evaluative judgment. This may be worthwhile but cannot be the *only* assessment outcome. See [[Assessment Design for a Time of AI — Dawson 2025]].

4. **Clinical disciplines**: evaluative judgment about the quality and safety of diagnostic AI outputs is a core professional competency for health professions graduates. See [[AI Education in Medical Imaging]].

> [!important]
> Evaluative judgment cannot be developed by simply *using* AI tools. It requires practice in making and testing quality judgments without the answer being provided. AI production tools (ChatGPT etc.) do not scaffold this; they provide the answer.

---

## Assessment Implications

Assessments that develop evaluative judgment:
- Rubric self-assessment with comparison to teacher assessment
- Evaluation of exemplars of varying quality
- Peer feedback tasks
- Interactive oral assessment (viva) — requires student to articulate and defend quality judgments
- "Critique the AI output" tasks — valid when explicitly targeting evaluative judgment as the outcome
