# Sonographer Competency

The structure of professional knowledge, skills, and attitudes required for safe and effective diagnostic ultrasound practice — and how that structure is being stress-tested by AI automation.

---

## The Competency Architecture

Australian professional standard, established by the *Professional Competency Framework for Sonographers* (Edwards et al. 2022 via ASAR). Four interlocking components:

| Component | Granularity | Purpose |
|-----------|-------------|---------|
| **Competency standards** | 77 criteria; 307 entry-level cues | Regulatory benchmarks; curriculum design |
| **Knowledge** | 125 entry-level items; 49 advanced | Domain-specific knowledge requirements |
| **Attitudes** | 16 consensus items | Professional character; relational practice |
| **Holistic competency matrix** | 40 cells (5 Dreyfus levels × 8 aspects) | Progression from novice to expert |

---

## Mapping Competency to SRK Levels

The holistic competency matrix implicitly encodes the [[Skills Rules and Knowledge]] hierarchy:

| Matrix aspect | SRK level | What AI changes |
|---------------|-----------|----------------|
| Psychomotor skills | **Skill** | AI guidance tools bypass physical repetition → may prevent S-level compilation |
| Standard of work | **Rule** | AI can check against standards; rule compilation still requires practice |
| Applied knowledge | **Knowledge** | AI provides suggestions; understanding of *why* remains human |
| Coping with complexity | **Knowledge** | AI is unreliable for out-of-distribution cases; K-level reasoning essential |
| Autonomy | **K→S** | AI dependency may prevent the autonomy that signals Dreyfus levels 4–5 |
| Perception of content | **Skill→Rule** | Pattern recognition partially automatable; expert perceptual gestalt is not |
| Attitudes to learning | — | Not automatable; may atrophy if AI removes productive struggle |
| Attitudes toward self/colleagues/patients | — | Irreplaceable relational dimension |

> [!important]
> AI disruption is maximally concentrated at the **Skill level** (psychomotor bypass) and **Rule level** (protocol provision). AI is weakest at the **Knowledge level** (novel case reasoning). The Dreyfus progression from novice to expert requires building through S and R levels to reach effortless expert performance — AI shortcuts that threaten to strand practitioners at a rule-follower stage that *feels* competent but lacks the depth of genuine expertise.

## EPAs: Competency as Integrated Whole-Examination Performance

Entrustable Professional Activities (EPAs) are the *operational* face of competency — not individual skills, but complete ultrasound examinations performed without direct supervision. Key principles (Edwards et al. 2023):

- Practitioners **acquire competencies** but are **entrusted with EPAs** — the trust is the supervisor's, not something the student possesses
- A single EPA requires simultaneous integration of all three SRK levels: psychomotor execution (S), protocol adherence (R), and clinical reasoning when situations deviate (K)
- **Routine EPAs** (e.g., 1st-trimester dating scan) are S+R dominant
- **Complex/emergency EPAs** are K-dominant — precisely where AI is least reliable and where industry consensus says new graduates are not ready

### ASAR EPA Structure for Graduate General Sonographers
Six critical practice units; graduates must achieve 5/6 minimum; on-call/emergency work explicitly excluded at graduate level.

---

## The Emergency Work Consensus as K-Level Policy

Broad industry agreement: new graduates should not perform emergency/on-call examinations. This is implicitly a policy about K-level readiness — emergencies are unpredictable, high-stakes, out-of-distribution cases requiring knowledge-based reasoning rather than protocol execution.

> [!important]
> AI cannot fill this gap. Emergency examinations are precisely where AI is most likely to be miscalibrated (out-of-distribution) or unavailable (system failure, connectivity). Graduates not ready for emergency work without AI are certainly not ready for it with AI assistance that may fail or mislead.

## The AI Competency Gap

The current competency framework and EPA standards were developed without reference to AI assistance. Several AI-related gaps follow directly:

**1. The simulation validity problem**
The EPA framework notes that simulated competencies don't transfer reliably to clinical practice. AI-guided simulation (probe navigation assistants, real-time plane detection overlays) may widen this gap further: a student achieving diagnostic images with AI guidance demonstrates a *different* competency than one achieving them independently.

**2. The S-level bypass problem**
[[Psychomotor Skills in Ultrasound — Nicholls et al. 2014]] establishes that psychomotor skill requires physical repetition and feedback-rich practice. Real-time AI guidance provides the feedback but removes the need for the physical execution that builds the skill. A student may progress through the competency matrix to "competent" without having compiled the S-level skill the matrix assumes they have.

**3. The atomisation-holism tension**
The framework explicitly critiques atomising competency into checklists — "overall ratings reflect competence more faithfully than detailed checklists." But AI performance monitoring tends toward exactly this: tracking discrete measurable outputs (plane detection success rates, biometry accuracy) rather than holistic professional performance. This tension needs resolution as AI becomes standard.

**4. The independence question**
The framework does not distinguish "performed competently with AI assistance" from "performed competently independently." This needs explicit articulation as AI becomes standard — possibly through a tiered EPA model (AI-assisted entrustment vs. independent entrustment).

## The Relational Residual

The 16 consensus attitudes of the competency framework — patience, humility, empathy, mindfulness, self-awareness of expertise limits, commitment to excellence — are precisely the qualities that AI does not supply. As AI progressively assists within Unit 5, professional identity concentrates in what AI cannot reach — which is not simply Units 1–4, but also the K-level and relational elements *within* Unit 5 itself:

- **5.2 (Procedure strategy)** — K-level clinical judgment: which approach, which probe, how to adapt to this patient's anatomy, history, and clinical question. AI does not determine procedure strategy.
- **5.3 (Safe patient care implementation)** — the *patient safety* dimension of implementation (monitoring patient response, managing discomfort, adapting mid-scan to unexpected findings or patient state) is human. AI assists with *acquisition mechanics* within this element, not with the care judgment.

The AI-resistant non-automatable core therefore spans **all of Units 1–4** plus the clinical judgment sub-elements of Unit 5. AI's footprint within Unit 5 is concentrated in **5.4 (documentation)** and the acquisition mechanics sub-elements of **5.3**, with a supporting role in **5.1** (pattern recognition that still requires human contextual synthesis).

These are precisely what [[Caring Relationship Between Sonographer and Patient — Van Der Westhuizen et al. 2020]] and [[21st Century Sonographer — Thomas et al. 2017]] document as already sources of professional stress and role ambiguity — before AI intensification. This framing resolves the "relational residual" question posed in the framework paper analysis.
