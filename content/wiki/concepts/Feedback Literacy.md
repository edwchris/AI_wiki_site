# Feedback Literacy

The understandings, capacities, and dispositions needed to make sense of feedback information and use it to enhance work or learning strategies. Coined and developed primarily by David Carless and David Boud; extended by [[Phillip Dawson]] and colleagues at CRADLE.

---

## The Process View of Feedback

Feedback is not a product (teacher's comments) but a **process**:
> "Feedback [is] a process in which learners make sense of information about their performance and use it to enhance the quality of their work or learning strategies."

Implication: if students do not act on information they receive, there is no feedback — only wasted effort. The key question is not "Did the teacher give good feedback?" but "What did the student do with it?"

> [!important]
> Dylan Wiliam: "Feedback should be more work for the recipient than it is for the donor." This is the ideal; it is rarely the reality.

## Student Feedback Literacy

Four-component model (Carless & Boud):

| Component | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Appreciating feedback** | Connoisseurship — identifying what is credible, relevant, and worth acting on |
| **Managing affect** | Making productive use of emotions; recognising that first-read affective reactions differ from action-planning reads; neither suppress emotions nor be paralysed by them |
| **Making judgments** | Critically evaluating feedback quality; exercising [[Evaluative Judgment]] about the quality of one's own work; *including deciding not to act on some feedback* |
| **Taking action** | Actually changing work or learning strategies in response — the only component that produces effects |

> [!note]
> A highly feedback-literate person consciously decides not to take on some feedback. "I wouldn't take feedback from anyone I wouldn't ask for feedback from." — uptake alone is not a valid measure of literacy.

## Teacher Feedback Literacy

Dawson & Boud ("What feedback literate teachers do") — 19 competencies across three levels:

| Level | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| **Macro** | Programme-level design; qualification frameworks; sequencing across a whole degree |
| **Meso** | Module-level design; timing, sequencing, and structure of feedback events within a module |
| **Micro** | Individual feedback comments on specific assignments |

> [!important]
> The **meso level** has the most leverage and is most neglected. Better micro-level comments are wasted if students never access them. Meso-level design drives uptake.

### Key meso-level practices:
- Front-load developmental feedback (invest early, when students can act on it)
- Separate marks from feedback (giving both simultaneously → students look only at the mark)
- Require students to self-assess using the same rubric; provide detailed comments only where assessments diverge (workload-negative strategy)
- Build feedback dialogues into assessment design, not just end-of-task comments
- Require specific feedback requests at submission; reject submissions without them

## Feedback Design vs. Feedback Provision

A common error: assuming that training tutors to write better comments will improve feedback literacy. It will not.

Feedback literacy requires the ability to function productively **even when the feedback is poor**. The goal is building student capacity, not improving feedback quality alone.

The lever is **feedback design** — upfront investments in assessment structure (rubric design, timing, sequencing) that scale independently of student numbers and reduce effort per student over time.

---

## Authentic Feedback

Concept from Dawson, Boud & Carless: feedback in higher education often lacks the authentic consequences of real-world feedback. In a job, you cannot ignore your supervisor's feedback. In HE, students often can (and do). Authentic assessment tasks should represent the feedback realities of the discipline.

## Peer Feedback

- **Peer feedback** = developmental comments on each other's work → strong pedagogical value; develops [[Evaluative Judgment]]
- **Peer assessment** = grades assigned by peers → contested; validity concerns
- Students tend to undervalue peer feedback relative to expert feedback — active processing requirements (what will you do differently?) can help
- Peer feedback is not inherently good: in close communities (e.g., improv comedy) it can be harmful and is sometimes explicitly banned

## Connection to AI

In an AI age, feedback literacy must include:
- Judging the quality of AI-produced feedback (is this credible? is this useful?)
- Knowing who or what to seek feedback from (AI vs. human; which AI?)
- Evaluating AI output as a form of evaluative judgment
- Academic integrity: distinguishing legitimate feedback-seeking from academic misconduct

See [[Assessment Design for a Time of AI — Dawson 2025]] for the AI-specific implications.
