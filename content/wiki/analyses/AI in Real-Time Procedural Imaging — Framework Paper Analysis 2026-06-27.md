## Analysis: AI in Real-Time Procedural Imaging — What a Framework Paper Reveals — 2026-06-27

*Generated by comparing a draft framework paper ("When AI joins the task: a framework for human–AI collaboration in sonography") against the full wiki.*

The paper proposes a task-centric framework for evaluating AI in sonography, built on five elements: task environment, task coupling, division of cognitive labour, calibration, and the downstream construct of verification load. Comparing it against this wiki's accumulated material reveals both its genuine contribution and its underexamined tensions.

---

### What the Paper Does Well

The core reframing — from "who performs better" to "how has the task changed" — is legitimate and grounded. The wiki's human factors literature ([[Ironies of Automation — Bainbridge 1983]], [[Skills Rules and Knowledge — Rasmussen 1983]], [[How a Cockpit Remembers Its Speeds — Hutchins 1995]], [[A Model for Types and Levels of Human Interaction with Automation — Parasuraman et al. 2000]]) all support task-system analysis over actor comparison, and the paper synthesises these into a domain-specific vocabulary for sonography. The "verification load" construct names something real that the automation literature has described but never labelled in this way: the continuous per-output evaluative burden of real-time AI integration.

The paper's empirical grounding is stronger than it acknowledges. The [[PROMETHEUS RCT — Day et al. 2025]] finding — that AI time savings cluster at moments when manual scanning was already paused — is the cleanest available evidence for the temporal fit claim: AI helps most when it aligns with, rather than interrupts, the task's natural rhythm.

---

### The Unresolved Tension at the Centre

The paper's framework assumes practitioners *perform* verification — the question is how costly it is. But the automation bias literature ([[Automation Bias Systematic Review — Goddard et al. 2012]], [[Automation Bias in Mammography — Dratsch et al. 2023]], [[Human-AI Interaction in Radiology — Kocak and Cuocolo 2026]]) documents that practitioners systematically *fail* to perform adequate verification, regardless of workload. The Kocak 2026 finding — trainee accuracy dropping from 80% to 20% under incorrect AI suggestion — is the strongest quantitative case in the wiki.

Verification load as framed describes a cognitive *obligation*; automation bias describes the systematic *non-fulfilment* of that obligation. A complete account needs both: what verification demands structurally, and why practitioners fail to meet it in practice. The paper does not distinguish these.

---

### The Calibration Bootstrapping Gap

The framework's calibration construct argues that appropriate trust develops through use. But if AI is introduced from the start of training ([[AI-Enhanced Cardiac US Training — Karni et al. 2025]] demonstrates short-term acquisition gains for AI-guided novices), practitioners may never develop the independent baseline required to evaluate AI outputs against their own reference performance.

This is the specific deskilling risk that makes calibration self-undermining: the mechanism that is supposed to fix miscalibrated trust depends on competencies that AI-from-day-one training may never produce. Neither the paper nor the existing wiki literature has named this "calibration bootstrapping" problem explicitly — it is entailed by the combination of Karni 2025 (AI-trained novices), Bainbridge 1983 (deskilling through disuse), and the paper's own calibration construct.

The [[Measuring AI Preparedness in Health Professions Education — Edwards et al. 2026]] finding that Cognition is the lowest scoring domain among MRS graduates entering clinical practice confirms that the current workforce is underprepared in the domain that matters most for calibration.

---

### The Relational Dimension as Undertheorised Residual

As AI handles more acquisition subtasks, the practitioner's irreplaceable contribution concentrates in the relational encounter. [[21st Century Sonographer — Thomas et al. 2017]] and [[Caring Relationship Between Sonographer and Patient — Van Der Westhuizen et al. 2020]] establish that this relational work is already a source of professional stress and role ambiguity without AI. The paper mentions the patient-as-relational-partner but does not develop it into a claim about what AI delegation *concentrates* as well as what it *removes*.

> [!question]
> As AI reduces technical burden in scanning, does the practitioner's relational presence intensify as the remaining irreplaceable contribution? Does this reconfigure professional identity around affective and communicative competency rather than psychomotor skill?

---

### State of the Field Implication

The wiki now contains three convergent 2025–2026 papers — [[PROMETHEUS RCT — Day et al. 2025]], [[Human-AI Interaction in Radiology — Kocak and Cuocolo 2026]], and Hsiao et al. 2026 (under review, not yet in wiki) — all approaching AI in imaging through a workflow/interaction lens rather than an accuracy lens. The actor-centric paradigm is already retreating. The contribution of this framework paper is to provide vocabulary for what comes next, but it arrives at a field already moving and should position itself accordingly.

**Key open questions this analysis surfaces:**
1. Can verification load be operationalised and distinguished from existing supervisory control constructs?
2. What does calibrated trust look like for a practitioner who trained with AI from day one?
3. Does the framework apply beyond sonography to all real-time procedurally acquired imaging (interventional radiology, bronchoscopy, surgical navigation)?
4. Is the relational patient encounter the structural residual of progressive AI delegation in sonography — and if so, what does this mean for training?

### Update: The Competency Architecture as Substrate — 2026-06-27

*Added following ingest of [[Australian Sonographer Competency Framework — Edwards et al. 2022]] and [[Entrustable Professional Activities in Sonography — Edwards et al. 2023]].*

The framework paper argues from task analysis but never grounds that analysis in the documented professional competency structure. The ASAR competency framework (Edwards et al. 2022, developed via Delphi with 55 expert panellists) provides exactly this substrate — and its structure maps directly onto the tensions the analysis identifies.

#### The Five-Unit Map

The competency framework organises sonographer practice into five units:

| Unit | AI Relationship |
|------|----------------|
| **1. Professional/Ethical** | AI-resistant; accountability and advocacy cannot be delegated |
| **2. Lifelong Learner** | AI changes what there is to learn; reflective practice requires productive struggle that AI may eliminate |
| **3. Safety Manager** | AI may improve routine safety but introduces new failure modes requiring active detection |
| **4. Communicator/Collaborator** | AI-resistant; the patient encounter is irreplaceable |
| **5. Sonographic Practitioner** | AI is most active here, but unevenly across the four elements: **5.4 (documentation)** most AI-amenable; **5.3 (implementation)** — AI touches acquisition mechanics (probe navigation, plane detection) but patient safety management mid-scan remains human; **5.1 (knowledge application)** — AI assists recognition, human applies clinical context; **5.2 (procedure strategy)** — robustly human, K-level judgment about approach, probe choice, patient-specific adaptation |

This map resolves **open question 4** from the original analysis: the relational residual is not merely a plausible hypothesis — it is what remains when Unit 5 is progressively AI-assisted. But that residual is larger than a simple Unit 1–4 / Unit 5 split implies: even within Unit 5, procedure strategy (5.2) and the patient safety dimension of implementation (5.3) stay firmly under human clinical control. The 16 consensus attitudes of the framework (patience, humility, empathy, self-awareness of expertise limits) remain the character traits of that residual. [[Caring Relationship Between Sonographer and Patient — Van Der Westhuizen et al. 2020]] and [[21st Century Sonographer — Thomas et al. 2017]] already document Units 1 and 4 as sources of professional stress without AI intensification of Unit 5. The claim that AI "concentrates" relational work is therefore empirically grounded in three converging documents.

#### The SRK Reading of the Holistic Competency Matrix

The competency framework's eight-aspect holistic matrix maps onto the [[Skills Rules and Knowledge]] framework with precision:

- **Psychomotor skills** → Skill level (S): sensorimotor, automated, acquired through physical repetition
- **Standard of work / Perception of content** → Rule level (R): recognition-triggered procedural responses
- **Applied knowledge / Coping with complexity / Autonomy** → Knowledge level (K): deliberate reasoning in novel situations

AI guidance tools operate primarily at the **S level** (real-time probe navigation, plane detection overlays) and **R level** (protocol compliance checking, flagging). They are weakest at the **K level** — novel cases, out-of-distribution presentations, clinical integration with patient history.

The Dreyfus progression encoded in the matrix (novice → advanced beginner → competent → proficient → expert) corresponds to a migration from K-dominant to S-dominant performance as rules and skills are compiled through experience. AI short-circuiting of S-level development (psychomotor bypass) prevents this compilation — leaving practitioners K-dominant at a stage where they should have moved to R and S fluency. This is not just the calibration bootstrapping problem in abstract; it is the competency framework's internal prediction of what AI-from-day-one training will produce.

### Update: The EPA-SRK Gap — Where the Bootstrapping Problem Lives — 2026-06-27

EPAs (whole examinations entrusted without direct supervision) require simultaneous integration across all three SRK levels. The EPA paper's finding on emergency work sharpens the existing analysis considerably.

#### Routine vs. Emergency EPAs as S+R vs. K Work

| EPA type | Dominant SRK level | AI reliability | Graduate ready? |
|----------|--------------------|---------------|----------------|
| Routine (e.g., 1st trimester dating) | Skill + Rule | High (standard presentation) | Yes |
| Complex (e.g., 2nd trimester morphology) | Rule + Knowledge | Moderate | Yes with supervision |
| Emergency/on-call | Knowledge (primarily) | Low (out-of-distribution) | **Industry consensus: No** |

The profession has independently converged on the same conclusion as the K-level analysis: new graduates lack the K-level capacity for emergency work. This is not because they fail competency checklists — by that measure they pass. It is because EPA-level performance in novel situations requires a depth of integrated expertise that atomised assessment does not capture. The Dreyfus model encodes this as the gap between "competent" (rule-following, proficient in familiar situations) and "proficient/expert" (pattern recognition, intuitive response).

#### The Bootstrap Gap, Sharpened

The original analysis identified a calibration bootstrapping problem: AI may prevent the development of the independent baseline needed to calibrate trust in AI outputs. The EPA framework sharpens this:

> The competency standards do not distinguish between "competently performed with AI assistance" and "competently performed independently."

This is not a minor omission. If a student achieves entrustment-level performance across all routine EPAs with AI guidance throughout, they may enter practice with:
- Full EPA certification across 5/6 critical practice units
- An evaluative judgment calibrated to AI-assisted performance, not independent performance
- No established performance baseline against which to detect when AI is wrong

The framework paper's verification load construct describes what happens *during* practice. The bootstrapping problem describes what happens *before* practice, in training. Together, they define a developmental paradox: AI makes it easier to reach the competency threshold, while simultaneously undermining the competency that the threshold was designed to certify.

#### The Atomisation Critique as Diagnostic

The EPA paper explicitly critiques atomised competency assessment: "overall ratings reflect competence more faithfully than detailed checklists." This is the Dreyfus argument in professional standards language — expert performance is holistic and non-decomposable. AI performance metrics tend toward atomisation (plane detection rate, biometry accuracy, scan time). A profession that adopts AI metrics as proxies for competency will systematically overestimate the readiness of AI-assisted graduates — because the metrics capture R-level compliance without capturing K-level depth.
