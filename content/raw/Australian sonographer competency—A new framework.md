## Abstract

Sonographers possess a unique and complex body of knowledge and skills, providing real health benefits to the community. The value of competency frameworks rests on their capacity to support and facilitate professional practice in the interests of public safety. The commitment to act in the service of others carries with it a professional and ethical obligation to practice within the boundaries of accepted competency standards. The well-accepted Australasian Sonographers Association (ASA) competency standards for entry-level Australian sonographers were introduced a decade ago. At this time, ultrasound technology, its application and the sonographer's role as a critical interface to this technology continue to evolve. The article provides an overview of research used to develop a contemporary competency framework for sonographers. It describes each framework component and how these components have a potential role in sonographer education and clinical practice at different levels of expertise. Sonographer competencies were developed via a national collaborative research project led by a multi-institutional research team of academic and industry professionals. A Delphi study design elicited consensus on multiple competency characteristics from an expert panel of 55 sonographers. The expert panel contained representatives from all Australian states and territories and represented all domains of practice. The resulting project outcome consisted of a competency framework containing four significant components: sonographer competency standards, sonographer knowledge, sonographer attitudes and a holistic competency matrix.

## 1 INTRODUCTION

Health professionals use competencies to describe the knowledge, skills and attitudes a person in the discipline should demonstrate.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0001" aria-label="Reference 1 - J Contin Educ Nurs">1</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0002" aria-label="Reference 2 - Educ Res Rev">2</a></sup> Healthcare professions have developed and adopted competency standards over the past decades.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0003" aria-label="Reference 3 - Educ Philos Theory">3</a> - <a href="#sono12309-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5 - Nurs Health Sci">5</a></sup> Heightened attention has driven this movement with a more proactive focus on risk management, consumer rights and workforce expansion.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6 - Med J Aust">6</a></sup> Competencies assist in establishing minimum practice standards, validate issues that practitioners consider essential, and provide direct observable indicators to assess professional performance.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Adv Health Sci Educ">7</a></sup> Competency standards also form the foundation of registration and credentialing processes for health professionals.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0008" aria-label="Reference 8 - Arts Psychother">8</a></sup>

In Australia, sonography is not a registered health profession.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0009" aria-label="Reference 9">9</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0010" aria-label="Reference 10 - Radiography">10</a></sup> Instead, sonographers seeking employment require accreditation by the Australian Sonographer Accreditation Registry (ASAR). The ASAR is a dynamic database of the sonographer workforce and works in conjunction with the Medicare Benefits Scheme.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0011" aria-label="Reference 11">11</a></sup> The names and accreditation type of current sonographers on the ASAR is publicly available on the ASAR website.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0012" aria-label="Reference 12">12</a></sup> A copy of the ASAR is held by the Australian Department of Health, which can audit individual claims to ensure only those services provided by qualified practitioners or students enrolled in a recognised ultrasound training program are eligible for a Medicare rebate.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0013" aria-label="Reference 13">13</a></sup> To be admitted to ASAR, sonographers must fulfil the requirements or enrol in an ASAR accredited course. The ASAR defines the minimum training standards and is responsible for the assessment and certification of sonography professional entry education programs in Australia. The ASAR standards for accreditation of sonographer courses <sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0014" aria-label="Reference 14">14</a></sup> recommends minimum clinical experience in Australia or New Zealand of 3 days/week over a 2-year period, and students achieve competencies as defined in the existing Australasian Sonography Association (ASA) standards for the entry level sonographer.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> The ASA standards for the entry level sonographer,<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> published in October 2011, outline criteria that describe an individual's practice from a novice to a competent sonographer using the Dreyfus & Dreyfus model.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0016" aria-label="Reference 16 - Bull Sci Technol Soc">16</a></sup> This well-accepted framework provides a benchmark to guide sonography education providers and workplace clinical supervisors regarding minimum standards.

Whilst the ASA standards for the entry level sonographer was relevant at the time of publication, the profession's evolution to include specialised skill sets, such as Point of Care Ultrasound (POCUS), sonographer led research, and adapting to new and emerging technologies, may introduce potential new roles for sonographers.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0017" aria-label="Reference 17 - Sonography">17</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0018" aria-label="Reference 18 - Ultrasound">18</a></sup> Notably, the ASA standards for the entry level sonographer cover criteria from student to entry-level sonographer (Dreyfus levels 1 and 2) and do not extend beyond this to include proficient, expert, or mastery levels.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0016" aria-label="Reference 16 - Bull Sci Technol Soc">16</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0019" aria-label="Reference 19 - IEEE Expert">19</a></sup> A well-researched, more detailed, modern standards framework that provides for sonographer levels beyond competency is timely. Developing a more detailed competencies framework is also valuable for pre-and post-accreditation curriculum management. Such a framework can guide professional entry-level assessment and inform a contemporary pathway for qualified sonographers seeking a return to practice or overseas professionals seeking employment in Australia. Clinical supervisors and educators can also use a comprehensive framework to develop consistent tools for assessing students, potentially reducing industry concerns regarding the skill variance of entry-level sonographers.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0020" aria-label="Reference 20 - Sonography">20</a></sup>

This paper introduces a new competency framework for Australian sonographers, providing an overview of each component and acts as a compendium to the full competency framework.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0021" aria-label="Reference 21">21</a></sup> (available at [https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.17148035.v2](https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.17148035.v2)) An overview of the project methodology and results are briefly described in this paper; a more detailed account has been provided in an earlier publication.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0022" aria-label="Reference 22 - Int J Qual Health Care">22</a></sup>

## 2 METHODOLOGY

### 2.1 Design

The collaborative research team consisted of representatives from three medical sonography professional bodies and eight current accredited sonography course providers. The ASAR provided an initial funding grant to support the establishment of the project. The University of South Australia human research ethics committee approved the study design (HREC #201916). A research agreement established written expectations between all parties and the funding body.

A Delphi methodology solicited opinions and consensus agreement from an expert panel (EP) of sonographers recruited from across Australia. The Delphi method used multiple, sequential anonymous survey rounds to obtain views from experienced individuals with specialist knowledge of the subject.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0023" aria-label="Reference 23">23</a> - <a href="#sono12309-bib-0025" aria-label="Reference 25 - Manag Sci">25</a></sup> An advantage of the Delphi process ensures face-to-face meetings are not required, reducing the risk of peer influence, and experts from different regions can easily participate in the study. The research aimed to develop a consensus-based contemporary sonographer competency framework that described the behaviours, skills, and knowledge sonographers should demonstrate at different learning and experience levels, including sonography students and accredited sonographers.

## 3 PARTICIPANTS

Fifty-five sonographers were recruited to the EP. Inclusion criteria required EP members to be ASAR accredited with at least 5 years of full-time post-accreditation experience and involvement in at least one of the following:
1. the supervision or assessment of ultrasound students
2. the performance management of qualified sonographers
3. the responsibility to receive or respond to patient or referrer feedback.
Exclusion criteria included employment in an academic-only role, member of the research team or non-current involvement in sonographic clinical practice or training. EP members were identified via professional networks and web-based advertisements distributed by the ASAR, the ASA and The Australasian Society for Ultrasound in Medicine (ASUM). Sonographers expressing interest in participating were provided with written project information and invited to submit an expression of interest (EOI) used to confirm eligibility and record demographic characteristics (geographic area, experience and working environments). A pool of Australian sonographers from diverse geographic and clinical settings was sought. After initial recruitment, any representation deficits were identified and filled by identifying additional EP members through professional networks and targeted invitations. All EP members provided written informed consent.

## 4 DATA GATHERING AND ANALYSIS

Six web-based survey rounds were administered to the EP from August 2019 to May 2020 using a web-based survey management platform.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0026" aria-label="Reference 26">26</a></sup> Each survey round opened for approximately 3 weeks, with short extensions granted on request. A three-week interval between rounds was planned. The EP was asked to indicate their agreement with competency statements on binary (yes/no) or five-point weighted Likert scales. Free text comments were also invited. Agreement responses and free text comments from previous rounds informed the development of subsequent rounds. The previous round's results were communicated anonymously to the EP to facilitate consensus. The literature does not define an agreed level of consensus; instead, cut-offs vary between 50%–97%, guided by the sample number, research aims, and available resources.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Technol Forecast Soc Change">27</a></sup> A 70% consensus was set for this project to provide an explicit criterion for the research team and operate within recommended guidelines.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Technol Forecast Soc Change">27</a></sup> A consensus was reached when 70% of respondents agreed or disagreed with the statement presented within a survey question. In survey round 1, the EP was asked to rate their agreement on competencies presented within a draft framework of detailed sonographer competencies. The structure and language in the framework were developed and informed by existing competency standards for sonography and related professions.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28">28</a> - <a href="#sono12309-bib-0030" aria-label="Reference 30">30</a></sup>

### 5.1 Sonographer competency standards

The sonographer competency standards provide a detailed list of competencies applicable across all areas of sonographic practice. The basic competency units and performance criteria, describe the required knowledge and skills for sonographers to perform effectively. Performance criteria were organised into five descriptive units; (1) Professional and ethical practitioner, (2) Lifelong learner, (3) Safety and risk manager, (4) Communicator and collaborator and (5) Sonographic practitioner. The five units, similar to domains of practice, used in other professional competency documents <sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0030" aria-label="Reference 30">30</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0031" aria-label="Reference 31">31</a></sup> provide the key themes essential to all competent sonographers in Australia.

Each of the five units contains further sub-sections called elements. Elements are the foundation competency standards attained by an individual based on their knowledge and experience. Table [^5] outlines the five units and associated elements.

The performance criteria are supported by ‘cues’, which provide descriptive, illustrative examples of minimum skills and knowledge required for qualified sonographers, but in some instances, offer examples to more advanced practice levels. Cues are not definitive, necessarily comprehensive, nor applicable to all situations. An example of a performance criteria nested within Unit 5, Element 1 is Apply knowledge of ultrasound imaging systems. An example of one Cue describing this criterion—requires skills to identify artefacts and distinguish them from actual structures. The term ‘procedure’ includes both diagnostic and interventional or therapeutic procedures.

During the first three survey rounds, the EP provided feedback on the applicability, including the wording of each performance criteria and their supporting cues. The EP reported consensus that all performance criteria be retained, including adding an additional performance criterion that described skills and knowledge pertinent to research, evidence-based practice and audits (Unit I – Element 6). There was also consensus that all cues be retained, except for cues relating to one performance criteria (Follow procedures to ensure delivery of correct pharmaceuticals to patient/clients). Several cues were reworded to meet consensus, based on EP feedback. At the end of round 3, the Delphi process identified 383 cues that described 77 performance criteria.

Survey rounds four to six aimed to determine if the cues used to describe each performance criteria reflected minimum expectations of accredited entry-level sonographers, or if they applied to more advanced levels. After round six, 307 cues met consensus as entry-level ultrasound practice requirements, 42 cues met consensus for advanced or specialised ultrasound practice, and 34 cues did not reach consensus. Cues that exceed entry-level expectations, and those which did not reach consensus are denoted in the complete document.

### 5.2 Sonographer knowledge

Sonographer knowledge outlines the knowledge required to enable effective practice in different domains of sonographic practice, including abdominal, obstetrics and gynaecology, superficial parts, breast, paediatric, cardiac, musculoskeletal, and vascular sonography. The ASAR determines the selection of relevant domains of practice, and this list reflects those currently identified by the ASAR.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0014" aria-label="Reference 14">14</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup>

A list of required knowledge for each practice domain was developed across survey rounds one to five. A list of 63 knowledge items was extracted from the existing ASA standards for the entry level sonographer.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> The EP provided an additional 148 knowledge suggestions. At the end of round 5, there was consensus that 125 knowledge items were minimum requirements for qualified sonographers, 49 were appropriate for advanced or specialised practice, and 37 did not reach consensus.

### 5.4 Holistic competency matrix

The sonographer competency standards, previously described, is a comprehensive document describing in detail the breadth of knowledge and skills of contemporary ultrasound practice in various clinical settings. The research team decided that a more generalised competency document focusing on universal characteristics fundamental to sonographic practice be developed as an alternative to the Sonography competency standards, as each would have different applications. The holistic matrix was created as a simpler, and less granular document to describe the expectations of a sonographer at incremental levels. Specifically, it informs sonographers' knowledge, skills, and attitudes required to perform across the five levels described in the Dreyfus & Dreyfus model of skill acquisition (novice, advanced beginner, competent, proficient and expert).<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0016" aria-label="Reference 16 - Bull Sci Technol Soc">16</a></sup>

The research team developed a draft list of descriptors for each level based on existing taxonomies <sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0032" aria-label="Reference 32">32</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0033" aria-label="Reference 33">33</a></sup> and delivered it to the EP for feedback. Throughout survey rounds 4–6, the EP provided input on the holistic competency matrix. After round 6, there was a consensus for competency descriptions in each of the 40 cells in the matrix.

## 6 DISCUSSION

This work describes a multifaceted competency framework containing four components that will guide best practice for sonography students, practising professionals of all levels, educators, and accreditation bodies. Sonographers possess a unique, complex, and manifold set of knowledge and skills, which they apply autonomously to optimise patient health outcomes. This involves simultaneous elements of advanced psychomotor, cognitive clinical reasoning, and communication skills.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0034" aria-label="Reference 34 - Sonography">34</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0035" aria-label="Reference 35 - J Ultrasound Med">35</a></sup> This framework describes the minimum skill thresholds that student sonographers should develop to be eligible for accreditation. It also provides milestone targets for accredited sonographers who develop advanced skills and continue professional development over their years of practice.

While it is internationally recognised that sonographer competencies should be defined,<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0036" aria-label="Reference 36 - J Am Soc Echocardiogr">36</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0037" aria-label="Reference 37 - Radiography">37</a></sup> we believe this research is the first that uses a rigorous evidence-based approach to determine those competencies. In contrast to the broad consultation process that led to the development of the ASA standards for the entry level sonographer,<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15">15</a></sup> this work utilised a contemporary research design approved by an independent research ethics committee. Initially developed and led by representatives of professional bodies and sonography course providers, the research strategy ensured collaboration with relevant stakeholders. The Delphi methodology using a consensus approach provided the research rigour that the results reflect the depth and breadth of the profession as it currently stands.

The four components of the competency framework provide for diverse applications and together capture the complex requirements of a sonographer in modern practice whose skill level cannot be measured by a series of tasks alone. The sonographer competency standards and sonographer knowledge are very detailed and can guide sonographic practice to a competent level or beyond. The granularity of these two components provides the detail required for regulatory bodies and the development and delivery of pre and post accreditation courses. The sonographer competency standards and sonographer knowledge complement each other, as specific knowledge is critical in attaining skills competencies. Moreover, knowledge across multiple domains may be required to achieve a single competency. For example, to perform an echocardiogram, a sonographer applies knowledge of human anatomy and physiology coupled with an understanding of cardiac haemodynamics and Doppler waveforms.

Sonographer attitudes can guide personal development and an individual's overall approach to professional practice.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0038" aria-label="Reference 38 - JAMA">38</a></sup> Education science literature provides examples of how attitudes can develop in tandem with the knowledge and skills required for a particular field or can be developed separately.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0002" aria-label="Reference 2 - Educ Res Rev">2</a></sup> The stand-alone attitudes in the framework act as a reflection mechanism of the importance of personal attributes and key areas of scholarship critical for sonographers.

The holistic competency matrix, which is less granular than other components, endeavours to capture the more complex and non-specific processes across the different levels of sonographic practice. It was developed in recognition of the argument that even detailed competencies fail to reflect the intuition and higher-order competence required for well-developed practice,<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0039" aria-label="Reference 39 - Med Educ">39</a></sup> and a focus on tasks and outcomes ignores the synergies needed for complex professional practice.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Adv Health Sci Educ">7</a></sup> The matrix describes eight aspects of ultrasound practice: applied knowledge, psychomotor skills, standard of work, autonomy, coping with complexity, perception of content, attitudes to learning, and attitudes towards self, professional colleagues, and patient/clients. Each proficiency level is cumulative, meaning indicators from lower levels are not repeated at higher levels, although they are still applicable. The descriptions for the three lower levels of practice (novice, advanced beginner and competent) are applicable to student clinical assessment and goal setting, and the descriptions for the two highest levels of practice (proficient and expert) lend themselves to performance management and career planning for accredited sonographers.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0040" aria-label="Reference 40 - Smith Coll Stud Soc Work">40</a></sup>

### 6.1 Limitations

We believe that the EP in this study represented the diversity of Australian sonographers by geographic location, practice setting (defined by case-mix) and service delivery models. Such a heterogeneous group is likely to have divergent opinions.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Technol Forecast Soc Change">27</a></sup> This phenomenon was observed for several competencies in this study, and as a result, the aim to develop a consensus based competency framework was not fully realised. A consensus was not reached, most commonly for opinions on the applicability of granular skills and knowledge as either minimum requirements or beyond minimum requirements. These diverging opinions were likely representative of those in the general ultrasound community. More survey rounds could have secured consensus, or consensus may never have been reached.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Technol Forecast Soc Change">27</a></sup> Further rounds were avoided to avoid EP fatigue and potential bias from repeated questioning. Future research could further explore the cues and knowledge items that did not reach consensus and accommodate expectations that may change over time. For transparency, items not reaching consensus were noted in the framework, to alert sonographers that the result was contentious, and decisions on the applicability of the competency should be considered within the context of the local practice setting.

Two stakeholder groups were not represented in this project; sonography students and patients. At the project onset, it was decided that these groups would lack the experience and knowledge to answer the survey questions. However, the value of these groups in enriching the description and conceptualisation of competence should not be diminished.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0041" aria-label="Reference 41 - Acad Med">41</a></sup> Further investigations using alternate research methods and tools are needed to investigate the perceptions of these groups. This could occur when the framework undergoes future and regular reviews, which are necessary for a dynamic and evolving health care environment.

The process itself was complex and at times, organic. For example, each round's results and EP feedback informed the questions in subsequent survey rounds. The competency framework was also updated after each round in response to EP feedback. It was challenging to complete these tasks in the inter-survey interval of 3 weeks, which was limited to minimise EP dropout and facilitate timely project completion. Managing the inputs of a large research team may have contributed to this. However, the research team's expertise was essential for promoting subsequent acceptance of the outcomes by industry stakeholders. Overall, including a methodology adaptive to feedback throughout the process strengthens the research design and is a key tenant of the Delphi method.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0042" aria-label="Reference 42 - Br J Occup Ther">42</a>, <a href="#sono12309-bib-0043" aria-label="Reference 43 - J Adv Nurs">43</a></sup>

Particular to this study, there was a risk of bias from the research team at the beginning and end of the process. At the beginning of the project, the research team developed an initial draft framework as a starting point for the first survey. This would have been undertaken in purer forms of Delphi methodology as an exploratory phase involving potential panellists using open-ended methods to gain insights.<sup><a href="#sono12309-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - Technol Forecast Soc Change">27</a></sup> The choice to avoid this potentially complex, multi-stage process, carries a potential risk of bias. This is also true for the final edits and formatting undertaken by the researchers in preparing the final framework for publication.

## 7 CONCLUSION

This paper outlines a new sonographer competency framework that encompasses entry-level sonographers' required knowledge, attitudes, behaviours, and skills and scaffolds extended scope of practice. It takes the form of four components: (1) sonographer competency standards, (2) sonographer knowledge, (3) sonographer attitudes and (4) holistic competency matrix. A robust and transparent methodology was used to clarify each framework aspect. Extending entry-level scope descriptors to more advanced knowledge, behaviours, and skills provides relevance to all practitioners. Implementing these documents and how they can be adapted to academic curricula and the clinical environment will provide interesting future research avenues.

The performance criteria are supported by ‘cues’, which provide descriptive, illustrative examples of minimum skills and knowledge required for qualified sonographers, but in some instances, offer examples to more advanced practice levels. Cues are not definitive, necessarily comprehensive, nor applicable to all situations. An example of a performance criteria nested within Unit 5, Element 1 is Apply knowledge of ultrasound imaging systems. An example of one Cue describing this criterion—requires skills to identify artefacts and distinguish them from actual structures. The term ‘procedure’ includes both diagnostic and interventional or therapeutic procedures.

During the first three survey rounds, the EP provided feedback on the applicability, including the wording of each performance criteria and their supporting cues. The EP reported consensus that all performance criteria be retained, including adding an additional performance criterion that described skills and knowledge pertinent to research, evidence-based practice and audits (Unit I – Element 6). There was also consensus that all cues be retained, except for cues relating to one performance criteria (Follow procedures to ensure delivery of correct pharmaceuticals to patient/clients). Several cues were reworded to meet consensus, based on EP feedback. At the end of round 3, the Delphi process identified 383 cues that described 77 performance criteria.

Survey rounds four to six aimed to determine if the cues used to describe each performance criteria reflected minimum expectations of accredited entry-level sonographers, or if they applied to more advanced levels. After round six, 307 cues met consensus as entry-level ultrasound practice requirements, 42 cues met consensus for advanced or specialised ultrasound practice, and 34 cues did not reach consensus. Cues that exceed entry-level expectations, and those which did not reach consensus are denoted in the complete document.
