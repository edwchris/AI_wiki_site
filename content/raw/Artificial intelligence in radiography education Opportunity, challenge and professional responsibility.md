Artificial intelligence (AI) has already established a clear foothold across higher education, prompting significant debate about its implications for pedagogy, assessment, and academic integrity. The rapid emergence of generative AI tools has challenged traditional assumptions about how learning is demonstrated, how knowledge is constructed, and how assessment can reliably evidence depth of understanding.

As the radiography field is grounded in evidence-based practice and clinical accountability, learners must learn to critically appraise AI-assisted outputs, check sources, understand context and apply professional judgement to any AI assisted work.

We feel over-reliance on AI for idea generation, summarisation, paraphrasing or problem-solving may weaken critical thinking, obscure authentic learning, and increase the risk of academic misconduct if its use is not transparent and appropriately governed. It cannot replace independent analysis, good research skills or proper citation practice, especially when relating to niche areas of experiential practice across radiography. Learners must understand that in education, as in clinical practice, AI works best as a supportive tool, not as a shortcut or a substitute for learning.

When AI is used to generate work and to also assess it, it becomes less transparent where human academic judgement sits in the process. This reinforces the need for a shift away from purely punitive approaches towards educational strategies that prioritise transparency, critical appraisal, and professional accountability.

Considering this, there is a clear need for community and knowledge exchange amongst radiography educators, clinical supervisors, and professional bodies. This editorial calls for a radiographic community of practice to share experiences of positive, ethical, and effective AI use in education, while also openly discussing where challenges and risks arise. Consensus on the degree of AI literacy, and best practice for academic and clinically focussed assessments at a profession level are required. This must lead to collaborative development of guidance, shared case studies, and best practice, grounded in professional standards and patient safety. By taking a proactive, profession-led approach, radiography can position itself not as a passive recipient of technological change, but as a discipline that shapes how AI is integrated into education and clinical practice responsibly. The question is no longer whether AI will shape radiography education, but how it can be integrated in ways that strengthen learning, uphold professional standards, and remain anchored in human judgement.
