## Abstract

### Introduction

Linking individual competencies to entrustable professional tasks provides a holistic view of Sonography graduate work readiness. The Australian Sonographers Accreditation Registry (ASAR) publishes a set of entrustable professional activities (EPAs) as part of its Standards for Accreditation of Sonography Courses. EPAs are distinct ultrasound examinations grouped within six critical practice units. This study reports on industry perspectives of current EPAs and their classification for graduates completing general sonography courses in Australia. The article also examines the value of EPAs and links their function to the assessment of graduate competency.

### Methods

An online survey tool elicited stakeholder feedback on graduate EPAs across six critical practice units and the potential for including a new Paediatric unit. From an original sample size of 655, 309 responded to questions about general sonography courses.

### Results

A majority (55.3%) recommended no changes to the existing EPA list, and 44.7% recommended amending the list. From respondents that recommended changes (138/309), all current EPAs received >80% agreement to be retained; in addition, nine new examinations received >70% agreement for inclusion at the graduate level. Whilst 42.7% (132/309) supported the current ASAR model requiring competency in five out of six critical practice units, 45.6% (141/309) recommended increasing it to all six. There was limited support, 11.7% (36/309), to reduce this number. Responding to the potential to add a new Paediatric specific critical practice unit, 61.8% (181/293) recommended its inclusion.

### Conclusions

The findings demonstrate that the current list of EPAs aligns with industry expectations. In contrast, there are divergent views on the modelling and grouping of critical practice units. The article's critical analysis of the results and implications provides stakeholders with a practical approach to clinical teaching and EPA assessment, and helps to inform any review of accreditation standards.

## 1 Introduction

Safe and effective health care requires a patient-centred, timely and accessible service. Achieving this relies heavily on the skills of individual professionals to perform at their best in complex settings. Sonographers are crucial in health care delivery, providing diagnostic imaging services supporting various patient types. The competence and experience of the individual sonographer heavily influence the success of each examination.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0001" aria-label="Reference 1 - Gynecol Obstet Invest">1</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0002" aria-label="Reference 2 - Ultrasound Obstet Gynecol">2</a></sup> The Sonography profession in Australia recently updated its competency framework via a large collaborative research project. This framework provides a detailed description of the components of competence, including the non-technical capabilities of the modern sonographer, such as professionalism and advocacy.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0003" aria-label="Reference 3 - Int J Qual Health Care">3</a></sup> The framework details the knowledge, skills and attitudes for effective sonographic practice.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0004" aria-label="Reference 4 - Sonography">4</a></sup> Although the framework defines the individual skills and knowledge required of the entry-level practitioner, it stops short of clearly defining which examinations should be entrusted to graduates without direct supervision. At the practice level, an individual combines several competencies to perform each examination. Defining these individual competencies is critical; however, it remains helpful for students, supervisors, course providers and the industry to understand which professional tasks, in the form of defined ultrasound examinations, new graduates should be expected to perform independently.

In Australia, course providers wishing to graduate sonographers eligible to work within an accredited diagnostic imaging practice must apply for course accreditation via the Australian Sonographers Accreditation Registry (ASAR). The ASAR utilises a contemporary model for course accreditation defined by their Standards for Accreditation of Sonographer Courses (SASC).<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> Within this document, a course must meet eight standards relating to Governance, Course Goals and Outcomes, Course Content, Resources and Staffing, Teaching and Learning, Assessment, Research and Scholarship and Evaluation and Review. A vital component of these standards recognises that students achieve a minimum competency standard benchmarked against an industry-endorsed competency framework.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a></sup>

To promote a model of work readiness, the SASC also defines a set of entrustable professional activities (EPAs) that each graduate must be able to perform, relevant to each speciality area. An EPA is an essential discipline task entrusted to a learner without requiring direct supervision.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0007" aria-label="Reference 7 - Med Teach">7</a></sup> In the Sonography context, once sufficient competence has been demonstrated, these are specific ultrasound examinations individuals entering practice can perform unsupervised. For example, listed under the ASAR critical practice unit – Obstetrics & Gynaecology, a graduate sonographer completing a general program should be entrusted to perform a routine female pelvic, 1st, 2nd, and 3rd-trimester scan at a minimum.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> The critical distinction between an EPA and individual competencies is that the individual acquires and is characterised by their competencies, whereas EPAs describe the work done.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0008" aria-label="Reference 8 - Med J Aust">8</a></sup> Individuals do not possess EPAs; instead, they reflect the level of ‘trust’ placed in them by a supervisor.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0009" aria-label="Reference 9 - Med Educ">9</a></sup> Sonography course providers aim to maximise the probability that a graduate can provide safe, effective patient care relevant to contemporary sonography, not just the ability to individualise competencies. Therefore, an essential task of graduate education is to develop sets of individual competencies synchronised with real-world professional tasks.

Given the recent publication of the *Professional Competency Framework for Sonographers*,<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0004" aria-label="Reference 4 - Sonography">4</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a></sup> this research was conducted to evaluate whether the current EPA list is contemporary. This article focuses on general sonography courses, reporting on the industry's views on the entrustable skills of a graduate entering the workplace. The article also discusses the critical role different stakeholders play in shaping new graduates.

## 2 Methods

The study utilised an anonymous online survey tool (Qualtrics) to collect data. Invitation to participate was circulated via professional Sonography groups and social media platforms. The online survey was open for 32 weeks from May 2022. Accredited Medical Sonographers (AMS; General, Vascular, Cardiac, Obstetric, MSK) or Accredited Student Sonographers (ASS) or interested stakeholders (academic, employer, medical specialist) over 18 years of age working in Australia or New Zealand were invited to complete the survey. Survey questions were structured around existing EPAs and knowledge items described in published Sonography competency documents.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0010" aria-label="Reference 10">10</a></sup> The research team pretested and agreed on the question phrasing and response format. Regarding individual EPAs, participants were asked at what stage (graduate or higher level) should an accredited sonographer competently perform each ultrasound examination. There were a total of 13 potential questions relating to general sonography courses. Four questions collected available demographic information. The survey design used forced-choice binary questions. This approach was preferred to improve test–retest reliability and reduce completion time.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0011" aria-label="Reference 11 - Ann Tour Res">11</a></sup> All data collected were anonymised, and personal information was not disclosed. The University of South Australia Human Research Ethics Committee approved the survey.

Data analysis included descriptive statistics of demographic and response variables. Fischer's exact test was used to evaluate the statistical significance between groups, and a post hoc pairwise test analysed associations between each combination. An a priori power analysis indicated a minimum 260 completed responses were required to ensure 90% confidence in reducing the sampling error to ±5%. This analysis was based on the total number of ASAR accredited Sonographers as of the 1st of January 2022.

## 4 Discussion

Multiple jurisdictions have endorsed and embraced competency-based, outcomes-focused education. Various concepts, assessment strategies and milestones are now entrenched within the health education sector to develop competent graduates.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0012" aria-label="Reference 12 - Med Teach">12</a></sup> What combination and how to effectively implement these, however, remains a topic of considerable debate in the literature.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0013" aria-label="Reference 13 - Med Teach">13</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0014" aria-label="Reference 14 - Med Educ">14</a></sup> The ASAR, via its SASC, offers a ‘hybrid’ model to guide accredited course providers. A competency framework provides a shared map for individuals working towards work readiness, and EPAs assist in the measurability of the competency-based approach. As ten Cate (2007) describes, EPAs are ‘the mass of critical elements that operationally define a profession’.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0015" aria-label="Reference 15 - Acad Med">15</a></sup> From a representative sample of industry stakeholders, we demonstrate majority agreement for current ASAR-endorsed EPAs.

EPAs are observable workplace-oriented activities requiring multiple competencies. Individual competencies, for instance, interpreting an ultrasound image, are just one element needed for the larger context of an EPA, for example, performing a 1st-trimester dating ultrasound. This ultrasound examination requires several competencies: communication, image interpretation, clinical analysis and psychomotor skills. The literature reports high-acceptability of EPAs by academics, clinical supervisors, educators and mentors.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0016" aria-label="Reference 16 - Acad Med">16</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0017" aria-label="Reference 17 - Med Educ">17</a></sup> The findings of this study have important implications and open debate on various topics, and here, we highlight some important ones as they relate to individual stakeholder groups.

### 4.1 Accrediting bodies

The ASAR is responsible for defining the number and type of each critical practice unit and the EPAs embedded within them. Our findings demonstrate that the ASAR should review its current model to ensure they align with industry expectations. This appraisal will require balancing stakeholder opinion to include all critical units of practice versus maintaining the five out of six model, coupled with majority support for adding a Paediatrics unit. In addition, any debate on the number of required critical practice units must include consideration of their embedded EPAs. A decision to embed particular EPAs must consider a student's ability to gain the necessary clinical experience and any potential impacts on patient care when a new graduate enters the workplace.

To add further complexity, in the current model, some EPAs are not exclusive to a single critical practice unit. For example, EPAs within the Breast unit overlap with those of Superficial Parts. Applying the current model, a student entering the workplace is entrusted to perform a breast ultrasound, either obtained via competencies acquired via the Breast or Superficial Parts units. A similar situation exists with the inclusion of female pelvis in the Abdominal and the Obstetrics & Gynaecological units. Whether by coincidence or design, this cross-over ‘protects’ these common examinations within the five out of six model. Students unable to reach competency in all six units are entrusted to perform breast and female pelvis ultrasounds, regardless of which combination they choose. Whether or not stakeholders widely understand this cannot be determined by the survey results. However, by labelling a critical practice unit ‘Breast’ but including breast ultrasound in two critical practice units may cause confusion. The course provider, the student, or the clinical supervisor may unwillingly not progress competency related to breast ultrasound if the Breast unit was not chosen as one of the five. The same could occur for examinations of the female pelvis if the Obstetrics and Gynaecology unit was not selected.

Taken as a whole, the results demonstrate strong support to entrust graduates with a broad range of examinations, which likely has positive effects on ensuring service provision. Medicare data reports that item 55,065 (Pelvis) is the highest claimed service within the general subgroup between July 2021 and June 2022 (977,944 services).<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0018" aria-label="Reference 18">18</a></sup> Breast ultrasound (item 55,076) ranks 4th with 554,827 services, 98% of which were claimed by patients identifying as female. Therefore, changes to any model must not inadvertently impact service access to women. Reports in the media have recently highlighted the issue of a gender pay gap for ultrasound services in Australia; notably, a scrotal ultrasound attracts a higher Medicare rebate than either a pelvic or breast ultrasound.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0019" aria-label="Reference 19">19</a></sup>

To address opinions to ensure highly trained graduates, the ASAR has several scenarios to improve its current structure. One option would be to retain the Breast critical practice unit for specialist breast courses only and highlight to general courses that Breast ultrasound is an essential EPA but sits within the Superficial Parts unit. Our results demonstrate no consensus that more advanced breast techniques should be included as graduate EPAs. Furthermore, it could be argued the Axilla, which gained strong support to be included as a graduate EPA, is a routine part of every breast examination. Considering the other critical practice units, such a merger would allow space to include a Paediatric unit within a new five out of six or six out of six model. Alternatively, a Paediatric unit could be added to the existing structure. Two paediatric examinations gained strong support to be included as EPAs, Paediatric abdomen (renal) and Paediatric abdomen (appendix). None of the more advanced paediatric examinations received strong support, which should afford access to training in most centres. Knowledge of paediatric normal and abnormal sonographic appearances and related techniques are considered “Core” within the most recent *Professional Competency Framework for Sonographers*.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0006" aria-label="Reference 6">6</a></sup>

### 4.2 Course providers

ASAR-accredited course providers remain ultimately accountable for the clinical assessment of students.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> Therefore, a set of published EPAs communicates to course providers the need to ensure work-ready graduates using robust assessment strategies. The ASAR does not dictate the assessment strategy type, scope or structure, and variation between course providers is expected, especially in the clinical context. All institutions will have their peculiarities, biases and resource limitations. Course providers, however, should be careful not to directly translate individual competencies assessed in simulated settings to work readiness. Simulation activities in healthcare still struggle to replicate clinical practice, and questions remain about the authenticity of acquired ‘simulated competencies’ and their clinical relevance.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0020" aria-label="Reference 20 - Instr Sci">20</a></sup>

A common practice within the competency-based education framework has been to reduce competencies to small units of behaviours for assessment, for example, via an Observed Structured Clinical Assessment (OSCE). As Brian Hodges articulated, there are dangers to the excessive quantification in evaluating human competence to a set of numbers.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0021" aria-label="Reference 21 - Med Teach">21</a></sup> This ‘atomisation’ can lead to trivialisation and threaten validity.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0022" aria-label="Reference 22 - Med Educ">22</a></sup> The practice may provide the perception of fairness in terms of standardisation of assessment which conveniently supports the course provider when necessary evidence is required to promote an unbiased assessment model. However, research has shown that overall ratings reflect competence more faithfully than detailed checklists.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0023" aria-label="Reference 23 - Acad Med">23</a></sup> Work-ready competence is not just dependent on small units of behaviour. It is a complex synthesis in which both the individual parts and interplays between these parts are required. Suppose one was to dissect the good sonographer. In that case, it is not simply the presence of the technology expert, versatile scanner, clinical interpreter and empathetic communicator that matters but some higher-order interaction between these traits.

Coupled with work-based assessment, educators must ensure assessments also consider the impact of trainees' competence on patient care outcomes.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0024" aria-label="Reference 24 - Teach Learn Med">24</a></sup> University ‘simulated’ assessments have their place but could fail to measure the competency required to impact real-world health outcomes. Traditionally, these focus on quantitative data, often at the expense of real-world validity. For example, assessments that focus too heavily on individual elements of scan technique rather than whether the student's combined effort answered the required clinical question. There is evidence that subjective assessment techniques are thorough, provided they merge strategies to establish their reliability;<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0025" aria-label="Reference 25 - Best Pract Res Clin Obstet Gynaecol">25</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0026" aria-label="Reference 26 - Med Teach">26</a></sup> thus, holistic work-based reviews, which rely on qualitative data, are valid.

Overall competence must be evaluated on a body of evidence rather than a single task and should include inputs from multiple observers.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0027" aria-label="Reference 27 - J Acad Nutr Diet">27</a></sup> EPAs should be capstone assessments critical to the ‘assessment of learning’ and communicate to both the learner and the student on work readiness. This is not to suggest these are solely end-of-course; to the contrary, EPA-based assessments can be embedded as milestones throughout the course, depending on skill level. Course providers are aware of the dangers and equity issues associated with enforcing strict hurdle assessments at the end of the course without appropriate scaffolding opportunities.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0028" aria-label="Reference 28 - Educ Psychol">28</a></sup> Furthermore, student discontent is likely if a reasonable time to develop necessary skills is not provided in the lead-up.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0029" aria-label="Reference 29">29</a></sup>

This research did not assess the ASAR recommendation that all students within accredited courses maintain clinical training of at least three days per week.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> Modelling of assessment, however, must consider the essential time element to scaffold students to assessment readiness. With the potential for adding EPAs or additional critical practice units, more research is necessary on the impact of clinical time.

### 4.3 Employers/clinical supervisors

Tertiary education providers in the healthcare sector provide the essential link between individuals and success in employment. Universities regularly promote their Courses' virtues as being ‘engaged’ or ‘partnered’ with industry to attract students. To this end, employers should remain active to ensure course providers are consistent with their promoted graduate outcomes and objectives. Course providers must provide annual feedback to the ASAR, informing industry views on the appropriateness of the curriculum and the graduates' ability to meet employers' needs.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0005" aria-label="Reference 5">5</a></sup> Industry should actively engage with stakeholder advisory forums and formal surveys on the ability of graduates to perform at the expected level entrusted to them.

Supervisors are critical to developing clinical learning, and this should be extended to a rigorous shared clinical assessment model between the course provider and industry.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0030" aria-label="Reference 30 - Ultrasound">30</a></sup> Holistic assessment based on EPAs using industry milestones will provide a more granular description of individual competencies and how these can be translated into work readiness. Engaged clinical supervisors should be free to assess with the understanding that university-located assessments do not somehow degrade or trump their feedback. Domain-specific knowledge is a central component of assessment success.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0031" aria-label="Reference 31 - Adv Health Sci Educ Theory Pract">31</a></sup> Even if clinical supervisors are not well-versed in conducting one-time assessments, their ability to provide a holistic view of a student's performance is essential.

University assessments and those performed directly in the workplace should complement each other. Care should be taken to provide supervisors with the tools to ensure evaluations focus on a complete view of the student. A ‘both/and’ approach communicates to the student the vital role of clinical supervisors in their work readiness evaluation. The advantage of the clinical supervisor is the ability to evaluate a student across multiple work settings and throughout the passage of time. Unlike other health professions, such as nursing, where faculty staff may be embedded within the clinical environment,<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0032" aria-label="Reference 32 - Nurse Educ Pract">32</a></sup> in sonography, the majority of clinical supervision and assessment is performed by experienced clinical staff. However, even without formal training, competency-based education supports the role of discipline experts as good clinical assessors.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0021" aria-label="Reference 21 - Med Teach">21</a>, <a href="#jmrs676-bib-0033" aria-label="Reference 33 - J Soc Work Educ">33</a></sup> As Ginsburg discusses,<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0034" aria-label="Reference 34 - Med Educ">34</a></sup> there are good reasons to ‘ *rely more on the subjective impressions of the experts, who seem quite readily able to make highly reliable judgements* ’.

This study also provides employers with essential insights into the ability of the graduate to engage with emergency and on-call services. There is increasing pressure on some departments to provide a broader suite of diagnostic imaging services at any time. A potential flow-on effect is pressure on new graduates to step into out-of-hours rosters upon graduation. The study highlighted broad agreement that recent graduates are not ready for these arrangements, and caution should be taken before graduates are rostered to these shifts before a base amount of time is first spent in a directly supervised setting.

### 4.4 Students

The student's clinical training environment will undoubtedly impact their ability to gain experience in specific examinations. However, it is essential to point out that learners acquire competencies but never possess EPAs.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0035" aria-label="Reference 35 - Korean J Med Educ">35</a></sup> In fact, every ultrasound examination is different and patient-specific, even when the same examination type is performed. What matters to the professional is their ability to combine the required competencies to succeed in the work activity. Therefore, individual competencies are transferable across domains of practice and entrustment is not simply related to experience in a specific examination type.

For individual students, entrustment is likely to follow from the demonstration of competencies in a range of core ultrasound skills such as communication, technical expertise and professionalism.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0036" aria-label="Reference 36 - Sonography">36</a></sup> In many ways, if a learner possesses the requisite competencies, the professionals' work environment helps dictate examination entrustment rather than simply a function of prior experience.

The relationship between the clinical supervisor and the student is essential, and the student has a significant role to play. Active engagement of learners in their own learning has long been understood as crucial to developing skills.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0037" aria-label="Reference 37">37</a></sup> Students must take responsibility for their learning and demonstrate to supervisors that they possess the critical attitudes required for entrustment. Research has shown that crucial characteristics such as integrity, humility and professionalism are essential practitioner attitudes.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0004" aria-label="Reference 4 - Sonography">4</a></sup> Further, a demonstrated ability for a student to deal effectively with unfamiliar situations will enhance trust.

In this study, students were less likely to recommend increasing the number of EPAs, which potentially ties to an emphasis on graduating rather than a holistic view of their education. Students, however, should not lose sight of the fact that individual professionals are ultimately accountable for their competency. Post-graduation professionals potentially operate in highly independent environments with limited supervision. Many smaller suburban or remote practices use a single Sonographer, and many departments and companies increasingly utilise intra- and extra-organisational teleradiology systems and services.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0038" aria-label="Reference 38 - AJR Am J Roentgenol">38</a></sup> Therefore, students should select courses with the best opportunities to meet industry expectations and not solely focus on assessment success. Engaging learners in reflective practice is likely to bridge this divide.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0039" aria-label="Reference 39">39</a></sup> Encouraging sonographers to think beyond the confines of the recognisable and embrace complex and unfamiliar examinations. A reflective practitioner can utilise inbuilt competencies and consciously analyse the unexpected to solve clinical problems. Such an approach will also likely benefit individual wellbeing and improve patient outcomes.<sup><a href="#jmrs676-bib-0040" aria-label="Reference 40">40</a></sup>

### 4.5 Strengths and limitations

Strong industry engagement is a strength of this study; the survey is well-powered, providing an accurate view of current industry expectations. The survey construction appeared to limit the potential for cognitive bias, with most participants completing the entire survey. From the cohort of 309 that commenced 293 also completed the final question relating to the inclusion of a Paediatric unit (95% completion). Online surveys are limited due to respondents self-selecting themselves into the sample. In our study, sonographers with interest in training sonographers may be overrepresented.

### 4.6 Conclusion

Training work-ready patient-focused sonography graduates is a crucial objective of sonography courses. In the Sonography profession, the performance of a complete ultrasound examination is an EPA and requires a student to develop and combine several general competencies. The results of this study verify accredited Sonographers, after completing a general sonography course in Australia, should be entrusted to perform a wide range of examinations across multiple critical units of practice. The study demonstrates industry endorsement of the current list of ASAR EPAs, providing clear evidence of the variety of skills and knowledge required of graduates and the importance of clinical teaching practice.

Accrediting bodies play a crucial role in constructing standards to meet industry expectations and patient care needs. Sonography as a profession continues to evolve rapidly, and our findings highlight a need to review the design and modelling of these standards. Our critical analysis provides a framework to examine the overlaps of EPAs across multiple critical practice units. In addition, the findings emphasise the industry view that diagnostic ultrasound plays an essential primary care role in child health and helps to inform the debate on including a specific Paediatric unit of practice for new graduates.

By providing a critical discussion on the role of various stakeholders, we argue that the assessment of graduates' work readiness should focus on EPAs and not on general competencies alone. Course providers should implement a practical EPA-based approach to evaluation. To achieve this, course providers should continue building links to clinical experts and industry and maximise a student's opportunities to gain clinical experience across various practice areas.
